# Why Femicide is a Societal Problem: A Comprehensive Analysis

## Understanding Femicide as a Social Problem

Femicide—the deliberate killing of women because they are women—is not merely an isolated criminal act or a result of individual psychological pathology. Rather, it is a deeply rooted societal problem that reflects and perpetuates gender inequality, discrimination, and systemic failures in violence prevention. To understand why femicide qualifies as a societal problem, we must examine the conditions that define social problems, identify the individuals and groups involved, and understand their competing interests [1].

## Conditions That Define Femicide as a Societal Problem

A social problem exists when a condition affects large numbers of people, causes significant harm, and is rooted in social structures rather than individual choices alone [2]. Femicide meets all of these criteria.

First, femicide affects a substantial and growing number of women globally. According to the United Nations Office on Drugs and Crime (UNODC), intimate partner/family member femicides account for a significant portion of homicides worldwide, with women and girls representing the majority of victims in these categories [3]. The World Health Organization reports that approximately one in three women worldwide experience intimate partner violence in their lifetime, and a considerable proportion of these cases escalate to lethal violence [1]. This is not a marginal issue affecting only a small segment of the population; it is a widespread phenomenon that cuts across geographic, economic, and cultural boundaries.

Second, femicide causes profound and multifaceted harm that extends far beyond the immediate victim. The consequences include psychological trauma for family members and communities, economic loss, disruption of social structures, and a pervasive climate of fear that constrains women's freedom and participation in society [2]. Children who lose mothers to femicide experience lifelong emotional, psychological, and economic consequences. Communities witnessing femicide become traumatized and lose faith in institutional protection systems [3].

Third, and most importantly, femicide is fundamentally rooted in social structures and cultural systems rather than random individual violence. Research demonstrates that femicide is systematically connected to gender inequality, cultural norms that accept male dominance, weak legal protections, and inadequate institutional responses to violence [4]. The World Health Organization identifies gender inequality and social norms accepting violence as key structural factors enabling femicide to occur [1]. This structural connection is what elevates femicide from a series of individual crimes to a societal problem requiring collective social solutions.

## Individuals and Groups Involved in Femicide

Understanding femicide as a societal problem requires identifying all the individuals and groups whose actions, beliefs, or omissions contribute to or perpetuate this violence.

**Perpetrators and Potential Perpetrators:** Men who commit femicide, as well as men more broadly, are centrally involved. Research shows that intimate partners or former partners commit the majority of femicides, and these men often have histories of controlling behavior, emotional abuse, and physical violence [4]. However, perpetrators are not acting in a vacuum. Their actions are enabled and normalized by broader social attitudes that accept male dominance, excuse controlling behavior as "passion" or "love," and minimize the severity of abuse [3]. Men who do not commit violence but hold beliefs supporting male control and female subordination create the cultural environment that normalizes femicide [2].

**Victims and Survivors:** Women and girls are the primary victims, but it is important to recognize that women are not passive recipients of violence. Many women recognize danger, attempt to escape abusive relationships, and seek help. However, their agency is constrained by social and economic structures that limit their options, including lack of economic independence, inadequate legal protection, social pressure to remain in relationships, and fear of escalated violence if they attempt to leave [1].

**Family Members and Community Networks:** Family members, friends, and community members play complex roles. Some enable violence through silence, victim-blaming, or pressure on women to stay in abusive relationships [4]. Others attempt to intervene but lack adequate support systems or institutional backing to effectively stop the violence. Extended family networks often have significant power in deciding whether abuse is tolerated or addressed [3].

**State and Institutional Actors:** Police, courts, healthcare providers, and social service agencies are crucial actors in the system. These institutions determine whether threats are taken seriously, whether abusers face consequences, whether women receive protection orders, and whether victims access support services. Research shows that inadequate police response, weak court enforcement, and insufficient victim support are systemic failures that contribute to femicide risk [2].

**Media and Cultural Producers:** Journalists, filmmakers, advertisers, and other cultural producers shape social narratives about gender, relationships, and violence. When media romanticizes controlling behavior, trivializes abuse, or fails to report on femicide patterns, it contributes to cultural acceptance of violence [1].

## Competing Interests of Groups Involved

Understanding the societal nature of femicide requires examining the interests of different groups—interests that often conflict and create conditions where violence persists.

**Perpetrators' Interests:** Men who commit or are at risk of committing femicide have an interest in maintaining power and control over women in their lives. This interest is not primarily about financial gain or rational calculation; rather, it stems from a sense of entitlement to control a woman's behavior, sexuality, and autonomy. When men feel their authority is threatened—through a woman's attempt to leave, assertions of independence, or refusal to comply—they may resort to violence as a means of reasserting dominance [3]. Some men also have an interest in avoiding accountability for their abuse, which motivates them to isolate victims, minimize harm, and intimidate witnesses [4].

**Victims' Interests:** Women in abusive relationships have primary interests in safety, freedom from violence, economic security, and the ability to make autonomous decisions about their lives. However, these interests often conflict with external pressures. Women may have an interest in preserving family unity, protecting children from losing a father, maintaining economic security dependent on an abuser's income, or avoiding social stigma associated with leaving a relationship. These competing interests can trap women in dangerous situations [1]. Additionally, women have an interest in being believed and having their fear validated, yet institutional systems often discount their concerns [2].

**State Institutions' Interests:** Government agencies have a stated interest in protecting citizens and enforcing laws against violence. However, institutional interests are often complicated by limited resources, bureaucratic inertia, and sometimes cultural attitudes within police and court systems that minimize gender-based violence [3]. Some police departments or courts may prioritize keeping families together over protecting women from violence, reflecting societal values that prioritize family preservation over individual safety [4].

**Society's Interest in Normalcy and Denial:** Broader society has competing interests regarding femicide. On one hand, there is an abstract interest in law and order and in preventing violence. On the other hand, there is often a conflicting interest in maintaining existing gender hierarchies and avoiding the difficult social changes required to prevent femicide. This creates a situation where femicide is officially condemned but informally tolerated through inadequate prevention efforts, victim-blaming, and resistance to fundamental changes in gender relations [2].

## Conclusion

Femicide is a societal problem because it affects large numbers of people, causes severe harm, and is rooted in social structures—particularly gender inequality and inadequate institutional responses to violence. Multiple groups are involved, including perpetrators, victims, families, state institutions, and cultural producers, each with different and often conflicting interests. The persistence of femicide reflects society's failure to prioritize women's safety over traditional gender hierarchies and family structures. Addressing femicide requires recognizing these structural factors and resolving the competing interests through comprehensive social change that prioritizes women's safety, challenges gender inequality, and ensures institutional accountability.
